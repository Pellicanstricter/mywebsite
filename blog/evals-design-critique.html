Building Resilient AI Systems Through Evaluation-Driven Development

Teams building with AI often don't have a reliable way to tell whether their agent is working. The tools are new, timelines are short, and there are few established practices for testing agent behavior, so teams often ship, watch how the agent performs with real users, and learn what broke from feedback after launch. In product terms, that is close to launching without user research: problems surface after release, when they cost the most to fix.

I've spent the past decade moving between architecture, UX research, and product strategy, working on everything from Google's workplace tools to healthcare enrollment systems to AI-powered design workflows. The pattern I keep running into is teams that pour energy into model selection and prompt engineering while almost entirely neglecting the thing that actually determines whether their system holds up under pressure: structured evaluation.

Andrew Ng's Agentic AI course offers a helpful way to think about this. He describes evals, which are structured tests of what an AI system produces, as the practice that most separates teams that ship reliable agents. In his view, a disciplined process for evals and error analysis predicts execution quality better than prompt engineering or model choice does.

That clicked for me immediately. Back in architecture school, I spent more hours in critique than at my drafting desk. The pin-up, the desk crit, the jury review: those weren't afterthoughts. They were the mechanism that turned ambitious sketches into buildings that could actually stand. The parallel to AI evals is almost uncanny.

What Evals Are and Why They Matter

Evaluations are systematic methods for measuring whether an AI system does what it should. There are three core types: code-based evals that programmatically verify outputs against known correct answers, human evaluations where people assess quality and nuance, and LLM-as-judge evals that use one model to assess another's reasoning and outputs.

If you've ever facilitated a heuristic evaluation on a UI or run a structured design critique, this logic is already in your bones. You define what "good" looks like, then you measure the work against that standard. Evals are the same discipline, applied to a different medium.

The mistake most teams make is treating evaluation as a launch gate, a one-time checkpoint. That's like running a single usability test and calling the product done. Systems that hold up under real conditions need continuous evaluation, the same way products that serve real users need ongoing research.

Ng's Four Agentic Patterns (And Where They Break)

Andrew Ng's framework identifies four design patterns for agentic AI. Each one creates specific failure modes, which is exactly why each one needs its own evaluation strategy.

Reflection is built-in self-critique: an agent reviews its own output and refines it before responding. Research from DeepLearning.AI demonstrates that GPT-3.5 with agentic techniques like reflection can score in the high 70s to 90s on coding benchmarks, matching or exceeding GPT-4's zero-shot performance. The system architecture outweighs the raw model. But reflection without evals is just an agent agreeing with itself.

Tool use lets agents call APIs, execute code, search databases, and verify information against external sources. Every tool call is a potential failure point: a timeout, a malformed query, a hallucinated function name. These need targeted evals.

Planning is task decomposition at the system level: an LLM breaking a complex request into subtasks before executing. It's the same muscle a product strategist uses when scoping a workstream, and I lean on it constantly. But without evaluation on the planning step itself, an agent can flawlessly execute a plan that was wrong from the start.

Multi-agent collaboration distributes work across specialized agents: one researches, one drafts, one reviews. The parallel to cross-functional teams is obvious, and so is the governance challenge. Every handoff between agents is a seam where quality can slip.

Observability and Human Review

Evals work best alongside observability, which means recording what the system does at each step: the input it received, the plan it made, the tools it called, and what came back. With those records, you can find the step where a failure started and fix that step instead of guessing from the final output. This works for any AI system, whether it is an internal research tool, a customer-facing assistant, or a multi-step agent.

In an agentic system, I put two kinds of evaluation in the loop. Automated evals, including code checks and LLM-as-judge scoring, run on every output and flag problems at a volume no person could review. Human evals cover the cases where judgment matters most, and those human decisions become the reference the automated evals are calibrated against. Each failure a person catches becomes a new test case, so the automated layer improves over time.

This setup helps with AI research tools, where every synthesized insight should trace back to the source transcripts. It helps with external tools too, where a wrong answer can reach a customer before anyone on the team sees it. In both cases, the step records show where to make the change and the evals show whether the change worked.

A Framework for Evaluation-Driven Development

This is the evaluation process I use when building AI tools. It works for a single-prompt feature and for a multi-agent system, and each step builds on the one before it.

1. Start with failure modes

Before you build, list the three worst outputs your system could produce, such as a wrong number in a report, an answer that cites a source it never read, or an action taken without approval. Write evals for those three first, because they are the failures that cost users the most.

2. Layer your eval types

Use code-based checks for anything with a verifiable answer, such as a required field, a date format, or a total that has to match. Use LLM-as-judge scoring for open-ended qualities like tone, completeness, and relevance. Use human review for the highest-stakes cases, and use those human decisions to calibrate the automated layers.

3. Evaluate each step, not only the final output

In a multi-step workflow, a well-written final answer can hide a wrong plan or a failed tool call earlier in the chain. Evaluate the plan, each tool call, and the intermediate results along with what the user sees. The step records from your observability setup make this possible.

4. Turn production failures into test cases

When the system fails in real use, add that case to your eval suite. Over time, the suite becomes a record of every failure you have seen, and rerunning it before each change shows whether an old problem has come back.

5. Define "good" with the people who use the output

Engineers can tell whether a tool call worked, domain experts know whether an answer is correct, and end users know whether it helps them do their work. Bring all three into writing the eval criteria and reviewing results, so the evals measure what the people relying on the system care about.

Getting Started

AI systems are moving from answering questions to completing tasks, and agents now search, compare, and take actions on behalf of users. As they take on more of that work, evals and observability let you check that each step was done correctly and find what to fix when it wasn't.

A first version can be small. Pick one workflow, write evals for its three worst failure modes, log each step the system takes, and have a person review a sample of outputs each week. Each failure you find becomes the next test case.
